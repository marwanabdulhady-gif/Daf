### Essential Question

### Visual Learning Bridge

The music director is trying to find the best way to arrange the chairs for a performance. The chairs must be arranged in a rectangular array. How many different ways can 12 chairs be arranged into a rectangular array? Use grids to show all the ways.

Pairs of whole numbers multiplied together to find a product are called **factor pairs**. Think about multiplication to decompose a number into its factors.

```
1 row of 12 chairs     1 × 12 = 12
12 rows of 1 chair    12 ×  1 = 12
2 rows of 6 chairs     2 ×  6 = 12
6 rows of 2 chairs     6 ×  2 = 12
3 rows of 4 chairs     3 ×  4 = 12
4 rows of 3 chairs     4 ×  3 = 12
```

1 and 12 are a factor pair.  
There are **6 possible ways** the 12 chairs can be arranged.

**Convince Me!** Critique Reasoning: Badr says, "Greater numbers will always have more factors." Do you agree? Explain.

### Visual Learning Bridge

Jassim wants to arrange her 16 action figures in equal-size groups. What are all the ways Jassim can arrange her action figures?

```
1 group of 16 figures     1 × 16 = 16     So, 1 and 16 are factors of 16.
16 groups of 1 figure    16 ×  1 = 16

2 groups of 8 figures     2 ×  8 = 16     So, 2 and 8 are factors of 16.
8 groups of 2 figures     8 ×  2 = 16

4 groups of 4 figures     4 ×  4 = 16     4 is a factor of 16.
```

The factor pairs for 16 are **1 and 16, 2 and 8, and 4 and 4**.

### Solve & Share

**Thinking Habits**
- Are any calculations repeated?
- Can I generalize from examples?
- What shortcuts do I notice?

**Look Back!** Generalize: Do you need to try all numbers from 1 to 24 to be sure you have all the factor pairs for 24? Explain.

### Essential Question

How can you make a generalization from repeated reasoning?

### Visual Learning Bridge

A new city park is opening. The gardener needs to select 15 trees from a nursery and plant the trees in a rectangular array. What are all the different ways the gardener can plant the trees?

**How can I make a generalization from repeated reasoning?**

I can
- look for things that repeat in a problem.
- look for shortcuts.
- generalize from the example.

To find all of the factors of 15, I divide 15 by divisors starting with 1. Then I use the Commutative Property to write two multiplication equations.

```
1 × 15 = 15  and  15 × 1 = 15
2 is not a factor
3 ×  5 = 15  and   5 × 3 = 15
4 is not a factor
5 ×  3 = 15  and   3 × 5 = 15   ← already found
```

When factor pairs start repeating, you can make a general statement, or **generalize**, that all the factors of a number have been found.

The gardener has **4 different ways** to plant the trees: 1 × 15, 15 × 1, 5 × 3, and 3 × 5 arrays.

**Convince Me!** Construct Arguments: The diagram shows all the factor pairs of 24 (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24). Use the diagram to justify the conclusion that when factor pairs start repeating, you know you have found all the factors of a number.

### Guided Practice

**Generalize**

Ustadh Marwan wants to arrange the 20 desks in her classroom into rows with the same number of desks in each row. She wants at least 2, but not more than 8 rows.

1. What are the factor pairs for 20? Explain how you know you have found them all.
2. Find the ways Ustadh Marwan can arrange the desks.

When you generalize, you find general methods and shortcuts to help solve a problem.

**Independent Practice**

Khalid invited 15 friends to his birthday party. They played a game where everyone had to separate into groups. Each group had the same number of children. The game could not be played with all 16 children in one group and each group had to have more than 1 child.

3. List the factor pairs of 16 and then find the different ways Khalid and his friends could divide into groups.
4. Why does 16 have an odd number of factors?
5. Can you stop checking for factor pairs when you find a pair that repeats? Explain.

### Performance Task — Store Displays

A pet store needs 3 displays with the products shown. The boxes of kitty litter (48 boxes) need to be stacked with the same number of boxes in each row. There needs to be at least 3 rows with at least 3 boxes in each row. What are all the ways the boxes of kitty litter could be stacked?

6. **Make Sense and Persevere** What quantities are given in the problem and what do the numbers mean?
7. **Reasoning** What are the factor pairs for 48?
8. **Be Precise** What are all the ways the boxes of kitty litter can be stacked with at least 3 rows with at least 3 boxes in each row?

When you notice repetition in calculations, you can generalize to help solve problems.

## Week 13 Notes

- Factor pairs come in commutative pairs; stop when a pair repeats.
- Square numbers (16 = 4 × 4) have an **odd** number of factors.
- Shortcut: you only need to test divisors up to the square root (when pairs start repeating).
