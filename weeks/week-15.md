### Solve & Share

Luqman has yellow tile on ½ of her kitchen floor. Write another fraction equivalent to ½. Solve this problem any way you choose.

Choose appropriate tools strategically. You can use area models or fraction strips.

**Look Back!** How do you know your fraction is equivalent to ½?

### Essential Question

What are some ways to name the same part of a whole?

### Visual Learning Bridge

Ahmed ate part of the pizza. He said ⅚ of the pizza is left. Karim said ¹⁰⁄₁₂ of the pizza is left. Who is correct?

**Equivalent fractions** name the same part of the same whole.

**One Way — Rectangle area model**

Draw a rectangle, divide it into sixths, shade ⅚. Then divide the rectangle into twelfths.

The number and size of parts differ, but the shaded part of each rectangle is the same. **⅚ and ¹⁰⁄₁₂ are equivalent fractions.**

**Another Way — Circle area model**

Draw a circle, divide into sixths, shade ⅚, then divide into twelfths.

Both Ahmed and Karim are correct because ⅚ = ¹⁰⁄₁₂.

A fraction has a **numerator** (top) and a **denominator** (bottom).

**Convince Me!** Reasoning: Majid ate ½ of a pizza. Majid ate ⅓ of another pizza. Did Majid and Majid eat the same amount of pizza? Explain. *(The wholes must be the same size.)*

### Guided Practice

### Solve & Share

Suppose you have a ruler showing fourths. Use your ruler to name a fraction equivalent to ½. Tell how you know.

**Look Back!** Model with Math: Do you think there is more than one fraction equivalent to ½? Draw a picture to explain.

### Essential Question

How can you use a number line to explain why fractions are equivalent?

### Visual Learning Bridge

Salman rode his bike ¾ mile to school. Name two fractions equivalent to ¾.

A number line is another appropriate tool for finding equivalent fractions.

Show ¾ on the number line.  
Divide each fourth into two equal parts to show eighths: **¾ = ⁶⁄₈**.  
Divide each fourth into three equal parts to show twelfths: **¾ = ⁹⁄₁₂**.

¾, ⁶⁄₈, and ⁹⁄₁₂ are at the **same point** on number lines of the same length, so they are equivalent.

**Convince Me!** The number and size of each part on two number lines are different. Can the number lines show equivalent fractions? Use the number lines above to explain.

### Another Example

You can use a number line to find equivalent fractions that are **greater than or equal to 1**.

### Guided Practice

2–3. Write equivalent fractions using a given number line.

### Solve & Share

Waleed bought a box of muffins. Four sixths of the muffins are blueberry. Write a fraction equivalent to ⁴⁄₆. Solve any way you choose.

What can you draw to model with math?

**Look Back!** How are the numerator and denominator of your fraction related to the numerator and denominator of ⁴⁄₆?

### Essential Question

How can you use multiplication to find equivalent fractions?

### Visual Learning Bridge

A librarian said ½ of the books checked out yesterday were nonfiction. What are some fractions equivalent to ½?

To find equivalent fractions, **multiply by a fraction equal to one**.

```
1/2 × 2/2 = 2/4
1/2 × 3/3 = 3/6
1/2 × 4/4 = 4/8
```

½, ²⁄₄, ³⁄₆, and ⁴⁄₈ are equivalent fractions.

Multiplying by 1 gives the same number. ²⁄₂, ³⁄₃, and ⁴⁄₄ are all equal to 1.

**Convince Me!** Critique Reasoning: Khalid said, “In each of the examples above, all you are doing is multiplying by one. When you multiply by 1, the value doesn’t change.” Is Khalid correct? Explain.

### Guided Practice

1. Use an area model and multiplication to show why ½ and ⁴⁄₈ are equivalent.
2. Use multiplication to explain why ⅓ and ⁸⁄₁₂ are NOT equivalent.

For 3–7, multiply to find equivalent fractions.

Leveled Practice For 8–13, fill in missing numbers.
