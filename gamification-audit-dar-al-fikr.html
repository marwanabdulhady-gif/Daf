DAR AL FIKR SCHOOLS · JEDDAH · GRADE 4 MATHEMATICS

Gamification Audit & Redesign

A review of the proposed Seerah-themed gamified maths course, and a motivation model designed for the specific students you actually teach.

Verdict in one page

The plan is thoughtful and the arithmetic in it is almost all correct. But there are three problems, and each one is fatal on its own.

  1. It contains religious content that must not ship. A visual depiction of the Prophet ﷺ, invented wealth figures attributed to him, spoils-of-war logistics as a points game, "theft of spoils" as a fun event, and a plague in Madinah. Any one of these reaches a parent and the project is over. This is not a style note — fix this first, before anything else.
  2. Roughly a third of it is not Grade 4. Percentages, Zakat at 2.5%, dividing fractions, probability, and decimal multiplication are Grade 5–7 content. Meanwhile four real enVision topics are missing entirely, including all six angle lessons.
  3. The gamification is points wearing a costume. Bingo, relay races, quizzes and badges. For affluent, status-rich, compliance-poor students this is the worst possible design — they cannot be bought with imaginary currency, and they will identify it as a worksheet in fancy dress within one lesson.

The recommendation: do not build this in Articulate Storyline. You already own a working 114-lesson engine. Keep the Seerah as the ethical frame, move the scenarios to present-day Jeddah, and replace points with status, choice and consequence. Details below.

1 · Religious and reputational review — fix before anything is shown to anyone

Take this section to your Islamic Studies head of department before the next design meeting. I am flagging what a qualified reviewer will flag; they should have the final word.

Must remove

In the planWhy it cannot ship
"Visuals: Portrait illustration of Prophet Muhammad" (Slide 1, Scene 1) Visual depiction of the Prophet ﷺ is impermissible. In a Saudi Islamic school this is not a debatable design choice. Delete the instruction entirely so no illustrator ever receives it.
"Prophet Muhammad has 500,000 dirhams and gives 123,456 in charity" Invented specifics attributed to the Prophet ﷺ. Fabricating figures about sacred figures to make a subtraction problem work is exactly what scholars object to. Use an unnamed merchant in Makkah instead — the maths is identical.
"Catastrophic Event: Theft of Spoils" This implies theft among the Sahaba. Ghulul — stealing from the spoils — is a specifically condemned major sin. Presenting it as a game event is indefensible.
"Catastrophic Event: Plague" in Madinah There is a well-known hadith that Madinah is protected from plague. Your Islamic Studies department will catch this immediately, and so will an informed parent.
The whole military-logistics chapter as a game — arrows per soldier, "Battle Planner," earning resources by solving multiplications Turning the Prophet's ﷺ campaigns into a resource-optimisation strategy game is a category error. The campaigns are sacred history, not a scenario to be min-maxed for points by nine-year-olds.

Must correct

  • Spoils division ignores the actual rule. The plan divides 1,000 coins by 5 groups. The Qur'anic instruction (al-Anfal 8:41) sets aside one fifth first. The plan managed to miss the one genuinely beautiful fractions lesson sitting inside its own topic — and got the fiqh wrong at the same time.
  • Zakat is not tax. The plan puts "Taxation and Charity (Zakat)" in one chapter and has students optimise both. Zakat is 'ibadah — an act of worship with fixed rules and a nisab threshold. Teaching it beside "tax rate is 5%, calculate the tax" flattens it into accountancy.
  • Honorifics and spelling. No ﷺ anywhere in the document. "Mecca" throughout; your school will expect Makkah and Madinah.

2 · "Rich, spoiled, don't want to work"

I want to be straight with you, because it affects the design.

That description is a staff narrative, and it is self-fulfilling. A teacher who believes a class will not work lowers what they ask for. The class meets the lower bar. The belief is confirmed. I have to design against that loop, not into it — and so does your team.

But underneath the label there is a real and specific design problem, and it is solvable. Here is what you are actually describing:

What it looks likeWhat it actually isDesign consequence
"They don't care about rewards" Extrinsic rewards have no scarcity value. A child with a driver and a PlayStation is not motivated by 50 imaginary points or a sticker. Remove points as the motivator. Use the one currency they cannot buy: status among peers.
"They won't try" Effort is socially expensive. In a high-status peer group, visible effort signals low ability. Not trying is the safe play — you can always say you didn't care. Invert the status of effort. Make choosing the hard question the high-status move, publicly, whether or not it is answered correctly.
"They're entitled" They are heavily managed by adults — drivers, helpers, tutors, staff. They have almost no genuine autonomy, so they assert it by refusing. Give real choice. A menu they select from, not a task they comply with. Refusal stops being the only lever they own.
"Homework means nothing" Many have private tutors. Completed homework is evidence about the tutor, not the student. Assess in the room. Process visible, live, unaided. Homework becomes practice only, never evidence.
"They're bored" Their baseline for production value is very high. A worksheet with a border does not register as an experience. Your existing decks already clear this bar. A Storyline quiz will not.

One more constraint nobody wrote down: the parents. Affluent fee-paying parents in Jeddah will complain if maths looks like "playing games." Every gamified system for this cohort needs a parent-facing artefact that looks rigorous — a standards report, a portfolio, an exhibition. Build it in from day one or the system dies of politics rather than pedagogy.

3 · Why this particular gamification will fail here

What the plan calls gamification is pointsification — the thinnest layer of game vocabulary over unchanged worksheets. Four specific failures:

The "catastrophic events" are not events

"A blockade forces Muhammad to re-calculate and re-allocate existing resources."

Nothing has happened. The student does the same subtraction with different numbers. A real consequence changes what you can do next, not the digits in the question. If the drought does not mean the class actually has less to work with tomorrow, it is decoration.

There is no decision anywhere in it

Every task has one correct answer, supplied in the plan. "Distribute the spoils fairly" is not a decision if fair means 1,000 ÷ 5. Games are made of choices with trade-offs. There is not a single trade-off in fourteen chapters.

The games are drills

Multiplication Bingo. Fraction Subtraction Bingo. Multiplication Relay Race. Division Bingo. These are timed drills. Your students will read them as a demand for compliance dressed up, and they are right.

Points cannot buy anything

"Earn points for correct division." Points that buy nothing, unlock nothing and mean nothing to anyone are worth nothing — most of all to a child for whom real points already buy real things.

4 · The same disease in the system I already built for you SELF-AUDIT

I should hold my own work to this standard. I checked the 114 decks. The XP layer in them is weak in exactly the way I have just criticised:

What the code actually doesWhy that is a problem
234 award calls across 114 lessons. 230 of them are a flat 25 points for clicking a chip. The reward is for touching the screen, not for thinking. Students work this out fast.
All four badges unlock on the first click of each type — every badge is earned inside the first lesson, in about ninety seconds. There is nothing left to pursue for the remaining 113 lessons.
Storage key is per-lesson, so XP resets to zero every lesson. No progression, no history, no reason to care about the number.
Exactly one award in the whole course is worth 50, and one is worth 15. Locking a considered estimate and getting a hard question right are worth the same as a click. Nothing signals what is valuable.
No class-level state, no teacher dashboard, no export. The teacher gets no information out of it, and there is nothing to show a parent.

What is strong in those decks, and worth keeping: the ten-screen Amplify Desmos arc, the teacher rail with anticipated misconceptions, "no public failure state" (wrong answers say not yet and open a model), the class-commits-before-grading card sort, and the two-named-students Connect screen. Those are real mechanics. The XP number bolted on top is the weak part.

5 · What to keep from the proposed plan

  • KEEP The narrative spine. A year-long story beats disconnected topics. This instinct is correct.
  • KEEP Weekly missions. A week-sized unit of work is the right granularity — bigger than a lesson, smaller than a term.
  • KEEP Interruption events. The instinct is right; the implementation is empty. Made real, this is the strongest mechanic available.
  • KEEP Cultural rootedness. Riyals, dates, caravans, Makkah, Madinah. Maths in a context students recognise is worth a great deal.
  • KEEP The hands-on activities. Fraction strips, base-ten blocks, area models — genuinely good, and they belong beside the screens, not inside them.

6 · The redesign — seven mechanics that trade points for status

Each of these is buildable into the engine you already have. Together they replace the XP number entirely.

1

The Sijill — a named-methods ledger

When a student produces a method worth reusing, it is named after them and kept for the rest of the year. "Use Layla's Method" appears on the board in March because of what she did in October. Your Connect screens already surface two named strategies each lesson — this makes the naming real and permanent. Peer status is the only currency these students cannot buy from home, and it is free to issue.

2

The hard lane

Every Show What You Know gets a second, harder version. It is optional, it is visibly harder, and only the hard lane can earn a naming right. Attempting it and failing is announced positively; not attempting it earns nothing. This inverts the status of effort, which is the single most important move for this cohort.

3

Commit before you see

Already half-built in the card sort — no grading until the class commits. Extend it to every screen: predict, commit publicly, then the model runs. A student who has committed is invested. A student who watched is not.

4

Consequence that actually persists

Replace the fake catastrophes. The class holds a shared budget that carries across weeks. A wrong decision does not lose points — it loses something the class chose to want, and next week starts poorer. One real consequence teaches more than fifty imaginary ones.

5

Table scarcity

Give each table fewer resources than it needs — one protractor, three number cards, a limited number of "asks" of the teacher per lesson. Effort becomes owed to peers, not to the teacher. Social debt is a far stronger lever than adult authority with these students.

6

Delete every participation point

All 230 click-rewards go. Nothing is awarded for touching the screen. If a reward survives at all, it is earned by a hard-lane success or by getting a method into the Sijill. Scarcity is the entire point — and these students are experts at spotting a participation trophy.

7

A real audience at the end of each topic

One artefact per topic, shown to someone who is not the teacher: parents at a short exhibition, a Grade 3 class they teach, a genuine school problem they solve — how much paint the art room needs, whether the canteen queue is fair. This is also your parent-facing rigour artefact. It solves the motivation problem and the politics problem with one object.

8 · Where the Seerah actually belongs

The plan uses the Seerah as simulation content — students role-play managing the Prophet's ﷺ caravans and campaigns. That is where all the religious risk comes from, and it is not even the strongest use.

Use the Seerah as the ethical frame. Use present-day Jeddah for the scenarios.

The Qur'an is direct about measurement and fair dealing — "and establish the weighing with justice, and do not fall short in the balance" (al-Rahman 55:9), and the warning to those who give short measure in Surah al-Mutaffifin. That is not decoration bolted onto a maths lesson. It is a measurement standard. It tells a child exactly why precision is a moral act and not a teacher's fussiness.

So: the value comes from the Seerah — amanah, 'adl, mizan, honest measure, fair share. The numbers come from the Corniche, the canteen, the school build, riyals and halalas, Red Sea distances — which is already the local context in your 114 decks. Nothing sacred gets turned into a scoreboard, and nothing is lost.

10 · A six-week pilot you can defend

Do not roll this out to the whole grade. Run it on one class, one topic, and measure it.

  1. Week 0. Religious review with the Islamic Studies HoD. Fix section 1. Pick one Grade 4 class and one topic already built — Topic 13 (measurement) is ideal because the honest-measure frame is strongest there.
  2. Week 1. Strip participation points from the engine. Add the hard lane and the Sijill. Baseline measure: in a normal lesson, count how many students volunteer an idea unprompted.
  3. Weeks 2–5. Run the topic. Table scarcity from week 2. Shared budget from week 3.
  4. Week 6. Exhibition to parents — the artefact is the politics solution.

Measure four things, not "engagement"

MetricHowTarget
Hard-lane take-up% of students choosing the harder question when the easy one is availableAbove 40% by week 4

If hard-lane take-up does not move, nothing else matters. That single number tells you whether you have changed what effort signals in that room. Every other metric follows from it.
